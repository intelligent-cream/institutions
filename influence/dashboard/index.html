How companies are matched

  • Each company is pinned to its legal entity identifier (LEI) from GLEIF and its SEC filer number. Chevron's parent has no LEI at all; Exxon and BlackRock changed SEC filer numbers when they formed new holding companies, and both histories are joined.
  • In each register, names are matched with hand-written alias lists and checked by hand. Subsidiaries count only while owned; spin-offs, joint ventures, trade associations and foundations are left out. Every match is listed in a matches file for audit.

How numbers are built

  • US lobbying follows the OpenSecrets convention (in-house expenses, or outside firms' income where no in-house report exists) and matches the official API on spot checks.
  • EU, German and French costs are self-declared in brackets; we use the bracket midpoint. EU costs refer to the financial year two years before each January snapshot.
  • PAC money comes from employees, not company funds, and is kept apart from lobbying spend.
  • Tax rates come from companies' own XBRL filings (SEC, or ESEF in Europe), pooled over 2019–2024. German filers are missing from the open ESEF repository, and Swiss companies file in neither.
  • Tax havens follow the list in Tørsløv, Wier and Zucman, The Missing Profits of Nations. GLEIF only shows subsidiaries that have an LEI and report their parent, so counts are lower bounds.
  • Currencies are converted with ECB annual averages.

What is new here

  • Earlier studies link lobbying to lower tax rates and more contracts in the US only, one source at a time. We found no study joining US and EU lobbying for the same firms with their tax rates, contracts and tax-haven subsidiaries, or counting haven subsidiaries from open GLEIF data.
